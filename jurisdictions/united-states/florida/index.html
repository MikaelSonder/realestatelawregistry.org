Executive Summary

Real estate law in Florida is the State and local legal framework governing ownership, transfer, recording, financing, leasing, use and development of land and buildings. It operates through county Official Records maintained by Clerks of the Circuit Court and Comptrollers, Florida property and contract law, local land-use regulation and a closing process commonly coordinated by title companies, escrow agents, attorneys, lenders and licensed real estate professionals.

A Florida property transaction normally requires review of title evidence, county-recorded deeds, mortgages, liens, easements, restrictive covenants, leases, surveys, tax status, zoning and transaction documents. The seller executes a deed, commonly a warranty deed or special warranty deed, which is witnessed, acknowledged and recorded in the Official Records of the county where the property is located. Recording provides public notice and is central to priority and third-party protection.

Florida does not operate a single statewide land register. Each county Clerk of the Circuit Court serves as the official recorder of instruments that may lawfully be recorded in that county, generally within a single Official Records series. County Property Appraisers maintain parcel, ownership and assessed-value information for ad valorem tax purposes, while Tax Collectors and local governments administer tax collection, planning, zoning, permits and development functions.

Cross-border transactions require coordination of Florida title, escrow, recording, property-tax, foreign seller withholding, entity, land-use and local compliance requirements. Foreign buyers can generally acquire Florida property, but a foreign seller’s disposition can trigger federal FIRPTA withholding. Florida also has transaction-specific restrictions and review considerations affecting certain foreign principals, agricultural land, land near military installations and other defined property interests.

Purpose

The purpose of the real-estate law function is to establish a reliable legal basis for acquiring, holding, financing, using, leasing or developing Florida property. It converts commercial terms into effective conveyancing and closing documentation, recorded interests and a property position that can be supported by title evidence and title insurance where applicable.

For an investor, owner or occupier, this requires analysis of the title chain, county Official Records, mortgages, liens, easements, restrictive covenants, leases, condominium or association documents, zoning, permits, environmental issues, property tax, documentary stamp tax and the correct county recording process.

Florida Characteristics

Florida real estate is defined by county-level Official Records, the central role of title insurance and closing practice, widespread condominium and homeowners’ association property, and local land-use controls. The Clerk of the Circuit Court and Comptroller records property instruments, County Property Appraisers maintain assessment information, and counties and municipalities regulate land use, building, permitting and development.

Official Records EnvironmentUnder Florida law, the Clerk of the Circuit Court is the official recorder of instruments that may lawfully be recorded in the county. Documents are generally recorded in one Official Records series.
Record ContentCounty Official Records commonly include deeds, mortgages, assignments, satisfactions, subdivision plats, deed restrictions, liens, tax deeds, notices, judgments and other instruments affecting ownership, transfer or encumbrance of real property.
Recording EffectFlorida follows a notice recording system. Instruments authorised or required to be recorded are deemed recorded from the time of filing in the Clerk’s office, and priority can depend on filing, notice and the applicable statutory requirements.
Conveyancing EnvironmentTransactions commonly use a purchase agreement, title commitment, survey, escrow or settlement process, deed, mortgage, promissory note, tax forms and Official Records recording.
Deed FormalitiesA Florida deed must satisfy applicable statutory formalities. County recording guidance commonly requires a legal description, proper acknowledgement, preparer and address information, and the grantor’s signature in the presence of two subscribing witnesses.
Title Insurance EnvironmentTitle insurers and title agents search county Official Records and commonly issue title commitments and policies for owners and lenders, subject to policy exceptions, requirements and underwriting conditions.
Property Tax EnvironmentCounty Property Appraisers maintain parcel and assessed-value information, while Tax Collectors and other local offices administer collection and related tax functions. The recorded deed and tax records should be reviewed together.
Planning EnvironmentLand-use and development controls are administered principally by counties and municipalities through comprehensive plans, zoning, land-development regulations, subdivision plats, site plans, building permits, environmental rules and local authority conditions.
Language ExpectationEnglish is the standard language for deeds, title, escrow, Official Records recording, tax, planning and court processes. Foreign documents may require notarisation, apostille, certification or translation acceptable to transaction parties and authorities.

Process Flow

A Florida property transaction is a coordinated title, contract, escrow, financing, tax and county-recording process. The property and local jurisdiction are identified, title and due diligence are completed, purchase and financing documentation is negotiated, closing occurs, and the deed and mortgage are recorded in the county Official Records.

1. County and Property IdentificationConfirm county, municipality or unincorporated area, parcel identification, legal description, title reference, zoning designation, association status and applicable local authorities.
2. Title, Survey and Property ReviewReview title commitment, Official Records, prior deeds, mortgages, satisfactions, liens, easements, covenants, leases, plats, survey, property tax and recorded notices.
3. Wider Due DiligenceReview leases, condominium or association documents, zoning, comprehensive plan, permits, environmental and coastal matters, building condition, flood zones, utilities, tax, insurance and commercial risks.
4. Contract, Escrow and Financing DocumentationNegotiate purchase agreement, escrow instructions, financing and security documents, representations, warranties, indemnities, disclosure materials, conditions and closing mechanics.
5. Closing and SettlementExecute deed, loan documents, mortgage and closing materials; complete escrow funding, payoff of existing liens, documentary stamp tax, possession and agreed settlement actions.
6. Official Records Recording and Follow-UpRecord the deed, mortgage, satisfaction, assignment, lien release, plat or other instruments with the Clerk of the Circuit Court and Comptroller, then complete title policy issuance, tax, lease, planning and post-closing actions.

Cross-Border Relevance

Florida is a major market for international residential, hospitality, commercial, logistics, industrial, agricultural and development investment. Foreign investors and lenders can generally participate, but Florida and federal title, recording, tax, withholding, entity, sanctions, land-use and financing requirements must be integrated into the local transaction process.

RecognitionRights in Florida real property are governed by Florida law and are publicised and prioritised through recording in the Official Records maintained by the Clerk of the Circuit Court and Comptroller for the county where the property is located.
Foreign Companies and IndividualsForeign purchasers and lenders may need United States tax identification, entity formation or qualification, corporate authority evidence, beneficial ownership information, powers of attorney, notarisation, apostille, translations and compliance documentation.
FIRPTAA disposition of a United States real property interest by a foreign person can trigger federal FIRPTA withholding. The buyer or transferee may have withholding and filing obligations unless an exception, withholding certificate or reduction applies.
Florida Foreign-Principal ConsiderationsForeign buyer transactions should be reviewed for current Florida and federal laws affecting property acquisition by defined foreign principals, including rules that can apply to agricultural land, property near military installations and other specified interests or locations.

International RulesFederal and State tax, sanctions, anti-money-laundering, beneficial ownership, financing, foreign investment and group-governance requirements may influence the wider transaction structure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where are Florida property deeds recorded?Deeds and other instruments affecting Florida real property are recorded in the Official Records maintained by the Clerk of the Circuit Court and Comptroller in the county where the property is located.
Does Florida have one statewide property register?No. Florida property records are maintained at county level. The Clerk records deeds, mortgages and liens in Official Records, while the County Property Appraiser maintains parcel, ownership and assessed-value information for property-tax purposes.
What type of deed is commonly used in Florida?Warranty deeds and special warranty deeds are commonly used. The appropriate deed form depends on the transaction, negotiated title covenants, seller and legal advice.
Does a Florida deed need witnesses?Florida deeds ordinarily require the grantor’s signature in the presence of two subscribing witnesses, together with a proper acknowledgement for recording. Exact requirements should be checked against the applicable statute and county recording rules.
Are planning and building permits State or local?Planning, zoning, land-development regulation and building permits are primarily administered by counties and municipalities under Florida State law, local comprehensive plans, land-development codes and building requirements.
Can foreign sellers trigger withholding?Yes. A foreign seller’s disposition of Florida property can trigger federal FIRPTA withholding. The buyer or transferee should establish the applicable withholding, exemption or certificate position before closing.

Practical Guidance

Before acquiring, financing or developing Florida property, identify the county, municipality and property regime first. Obtain a title commitment, Official Records documents, survey, Property Appraiser and tax information, zoning and permit records and, where applicable, condominium or homeowners’ association materials. The purchase agreement, escrow, deed formalities, mortgage, documentary stamp tax, FIRPTA, county recording, financing and local development controls should be managed as one coordinated workstream.

Preparation checklist: Identify the Florida county and municipality; confirm parcel number, legal description and title reference; obtain title commitment, Official Records, survey, appraisal and tax information; identify mortgages, liens, easements, restrictive covenants, leases, condominium or association obligations and restrictions; review zoning, comprehensive plan, floodplain, coastal, environmental, permits and building status; determine the transaction structure; verify signing authority and foreign-party documentation; assess deed witness and notarial requirements, FIRPTA and current foreign-principal issues; prepare escrow, deed, financing, tax and recording materials; and align legal, tax, technical, planning, title and financing workstreams.
