Executive Summary

Real estate law in Massachusetts is the State and local legal framework governing ownership, transfer, recording, registration, financing, leasing, use and development of land and buildings. It operates through 21 Registry of Deeds districts, Massachusetts property and contract law, the Land Court registered-land system, municipal planning and zoning powers, and a conveyancing process commonly coordinated by attorneys, title insurers, lenders, closing agents and licensed real estate professionals.

A Massachusetts property transaction normally requires review of title evidence, Registry of Deeds records or Land Court registered-land records, deeds, mortgages, liens, easements, restrictive covenants, leases, surveys, tax status, zoning and transaction documents. The seller executes a deed, commonly a quitclaim deed with covenants, warranty deed or another appropriate deed form, which is delivered at closing and recorded or registered in the Registry district where the property is located. Recording or registration is central to notice, priority and title protection.

Massachusetts has two distinct title systems: recorded land, filed in the Registry of Deeds, and registered land, administered through Land Court registered-land districts housed within the Registries of Deeds. Most properties are recorded land. Each Registry assigns recorded documents a book and page number or document number, while registered land requires compliance with Land Court certificate-of-title procedures. The Massachusetts Land Records system provides public access to Registry record information.

Cross-border transactions require coordination of Massachusetts title, closing, Registry or Land Court filing, deeds excise, entity, withholding, land-use and local compliance requirements. Foreign buyers can generally acquire Massachusetts property, but a foreign seller’s disposition can trigger federal FIRPTA withholding. Massachusetts income-tax, nonresident seller, transfer-tax, coastal, historic, environmental, condominium and local development requirements should be assessed before closing.

Massachusetts Characteristics

Massachusetts real estate is distinguished by its 21 Registry of Deeds districts, the coexistence of recorded land and Land Court registered land, deeds excise paid through the Registry, and local planning and zoning. The Register of Deeds records and indexes documents about property ownership within each district. Most property is recorded land; registered land is governed by a certificate-of-title system through Land Court districts housed within Registries of Deeds.

Registry District EnvironmentMassachusetts is divided into 21 Registry of Deeds districts, each led by an elected Register of Deeds and responsible for recording, indexing and preserving documents concerning real estate ownership within its district.
Recorded LandRecorded land is the prevailing title system. Deeds, mortgages, liens, easements and related instruments are filed in the Registry of Deeds, and recorded documents receive a book-and-page or document-number reference.
Registered LandRegistered land is filed through Land Court registered-land districts housed in the Registries of Deeds. Title is reflected through Land Court certificates and memorials, and transfers, mortgages and encumbrances require procedures appropriate to registered land.
Registry AccessMassachusetts Land Records provides online access to Registry record information across the Commonwealth. The relevant Registry remains the authority for local policies, certified copies and document-specific recording or registration questions.
Deeds Excise EnvironmentMassachusetts deeds excise is paid through the Registry of Deeds in the jurisdiction where the property is located. The person making or signing the deed is generally responsible for the excise, evidenced by stamps or the applicable Registry process.
Recording EffectRecording in the appropriate Registry provides public notice for recorded land. Registered land follows the Land Court registration system, where the certificate-of-title framework governs the registered title position.
Conveyancing EnvironmentTransactions commonly use a purchase and sale agreement, attorney review, title examination, survey, closing or escrow process, deed, mortgage, title policy, deeds excise and Registry or Land Court filing.
Title Insurance EnvironmentTitle insurers and title agencies examine Registry and, where applicable, Land Court records and commonly issue title commitments and policies for owners and lenders, subject to policy exceptions, requirements and underwriting conditions.
Planning and Development EnvironmentMunicipalities exercise core planning, zoning, subdivision and development powers under Massachusetts law. Planning boards, zoning boards of appeal, conservation commissions, historic commissions and building departments can each be central to a project.
Language ExpectationEnglish is the standard language for deeds, Registry and Land Court filings, closing, tax, planning and court processes. Foreign documents may require notarisation, apostille, certification or translation acceptable to transaction parties and authorities.

Process Flow

A Massachusetts property transaction is a coordinated title, purchase-and-sale, attorney, closing, financing, deeds-excise and Registry or Land Court process. The property and title system are identified, title and due diligence are completed, transaction and financing documents are negotiated, closing occurs, and the deed and mortgage are recorded or registered through the proper Registry district.

1. Registry District and Property IdentificationConfirm the Registry district, municipality, parcel or assessor reference, legal description, title reference, whether land is recorded or registered, zoning designation, association status and applicable local authorities.
2. Title, Land Court, Survey and Property ReviewReview Registry records, Land Court certificate where applicable, title commitment, prior deeds, mortgages, discharges, liens, easements, covenants, leases, plans, survey, property-tax status and recorded or registered notices.
3. Wider Due DiligenceReview leases, condominium or association documents, zoning, subdivision, permits, wetlands, environmental and historic matters, building condition, flood risk, utilities, tax, insurance and commercial risks.
4. Agreement, Closing and Financing DocumentationNegotiate purchase and sale agreement, attorney review issues, closing or escrow arrangements, financing and security documents, representations, warranties, disclosures, conditions and closing mechanics.
5. Deeds Excise and SettlementDetermine deeds excise, exemptions and Registry requirements; execute and deliver the deed, loan documents, mortgage and closing materials; complete payment, lien payoff, withholding analysis, possession and settlement.
6. Recording or Registration and Follow-UpRecord the deed, mortgage, discharge, assignment, easement or other instruments with the Registry of Deeds, or register appropriate instruments in the Land Court system; then complete title policy issuance, tax, lease, planning and post-closing actions.

Operating Constraints and Risks

Recorded vs. Registered Land RiskMassachusetts has two title systems. Recorded land and registered land require different investigation and transfer procedures. Treating a registered-land transaction as ordinary recorded land can delay or impair the transaction.
Registry District RiskRecording is Registry-district specific. The correct Registry must be identified by property location. Incorrect office selection, incomplete filing or failure to follow Registry-specific requirements can delay or impair recording.
Deeds Excise RiskDeeds excise is payable through the Registry of Deeds in the jurisdiction where the property is located. The applicable rate, consideration, exemption, stamp or Registry process must be established before filing.
Title and Assessment Record RiskRegistry or Land Court records and municipal assessment or tax records serve different functions. Incomplete review of deeds, certificates, mortgages, liens, title commitment, survey, assessment, tax and association records can leave material risks unidentified.
Condominium and Association RiskCondominium documents, trust or association obligations, assessments, board approvals, rental restrictions, reserves, insurance and governing documents can materially affect transferability, financing, cost and use.

Tax and Withholding RiskDeeds excise, property-tax issues, municipal tax liens, federal FIRPTA and Massachusetts nonresident seller tax requirements can affect cost, timing, priority and closing documentation.
Financing and Lien RiskMortgages, mechanic’s liens, tax liens, judgment liens, condominium liens, subordinations, lender conditions and recorded or registered restrictions can affect transferability, financing and priority.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where are Massachusetts property deeds recorded?Deeds for recorded land are filed with the Registry of Deeds in the Registry district where the property is located. Massachusetts has 21 Registry districts. Registered-land deeds are processed through the applicable Land Court registered-land system housed within the Registry.
Does Massachusetts have one statewide deed register?No. Massachusetts has 21 Registry of Deeds districts. Massachusetts Land Records provides statewide access to record information, but each Registry remains responsible for documents within its district.
What is the difference between recorded land and registered land?Recorded land is filed in the Registry of Deeds and is the principal system for most Massachusetts property. Registered land is governed through Land Court certificates of title and has distinct registration procedures for transfers, mortgages and encumbrances.
What is Massachusetts deeds excise?Deeds excise is a tax on qualifying deeds and instruments conveying real estate. It is paid through the Registry of Deeds where the property is located, generally by the person making or signing the deed, through stamps or the applicable Registry process.
What type of deed is commonly used in Massachusetts?Quitclaim deeds with covenants and warranty deeds are commonly used. The appropriate form depends on the transaction, title status, recorded or registered land system and legal advice.
Can foreign sellers trigger withholding?Yes. Foreign sellers can trigger federal FIRPTA withholding. Nonresident sellers can also have Massachusetts income-tax, estimated-payment, withholding, deeds-excise or related filing considerations; the applicable position should be confirmed before closing.

Practical Guidance

Before acquiring, financing or developing Massachusetts property, identify the Registry district and municipality first, then determine whether the property is recorded land or registered land. Obtain title evidence, Registry or Land Court records, survey or plan information, municipal assessment and tax information, zoning and permit records and, where appropriate, condominium, wetlands, environmental or historic materials. The purchase and sale agreement, closing, deed, mortgage, deeds excise, FIRPTA, Registry or Land Court filing, financing and local development controls should be managed as one coordinated workstream.

Preparation checklist: Identify the Massachusetts Registry district and municipality; determine whether the property is recorded or registered land; obtain title commitment, Registry records or Land Court certificate, survey, plan and assessor information; identify mortgages, liens, easements, covenants, leases, condominium obligations and restrictions; review property tax, deeds excise, zoning, subdivision, wetlands, environmental, historic, permit and building status; determine the transaction structure; verify signing authority and foreign-party documentation; assess FIRPTA and Massachusetts seller tax issues; prepare agreement, closing, deed, tax, financing and Registry or Land Court filing materials; and align legal, tax, technical, planning, title and financing workstreams.
